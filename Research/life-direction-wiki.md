## Executive Summary

*(Self-contained; suitable for forwarding.)*

**I'm at a genuine inflection point, not a vague one.** Financially secure, 61, in excellent health, kids launched, parents passed, and not interested in retirement — the constraint isn't opportunity, it's focus. Thirty years of incubating, advising, founding, and funding technology companies (100+ of them) plus non-profit and political board work gives a wide aperture; the current task is narrowing it, not widening it.

**The test for any direction is four traits, not one big idea:** something I'm uniquely positioned to do, that creates leverage through institutions/technology/talent in combination, that can survive beyond my own involvement, and that stays fun — meaning real time in the creative act, not administering a portfolio. The failure modes to guard against are named explicitly: busyness without direction, chasing money or status, and both saying yes too fast *and* failing to decide.

**Five problems keep pulling at my attention:** the loneliness/civic-institution collapse, the erosion of trust in institutions and information (and what that means once AI-mediated answers replace an open, indexable internet), the mismatch between higher education's cost and what an AI-saturated economy actually requires, the question of whether and how a given technology's pace can deliberately be accelerated or slowed, and the energy/infrastructure demands of scaling AI itself.

**Three experiments are already running, not hypothetical:** the Bell AI Fellowship's spinouts (Storyline fundraising, a voice-agent "vibe coder" in prototype, an "Expert OS" agent concept), a six-week paid cohort ("AI Dojo") built with a co-founder to take AI-curious professionals to high fluency, and a great-books-style seminar (*The Great Questions of Artificial Intelligence*) that uses AI as a lens on centuries-old questions about mind, knowledge, and society. Each is a low-cost way to test a direction before over-committing to it.

**The near-term goal isn't to pick a lane for the next decade — it's to run a short list of career sprints** (bounded, weeks-not-years tests) that convert "this appeals to me" into "I know whether this deserves more of my time." Education and civic/loneliness work currently have live sprint candidates; innovation theory and AI-and-democracy are appealing but less scoped.

**The timing argument matters too.** The same week this document was last revised, Google restructured Google DeepMind's leadership — Demis Hassabis stepping back as CEO, several long-tenured research leads departing to start independent ventures — a visible reminder that even the largest labs are not fixed points right now. Industry structure is unusually fluid; that favors an independent operator willing to move fast on a narrow bet over someone waiting for a stable role to commit to.

## 1. Context: Why Now

I am at a transition point. I am financially secure, 61 years old, and in excellent health. My kids are launched. My parents have passed. I have a window to do something meaningful and I'm not interested in retirement.

Society is at a transition point too, because of technology revolutions — especially AI — layered on instability in politics, economics, and climate.

**Background:** I most recently co-founded and ran the Bell AI Fellowship, a three-month program focused on voice AI applications. It incubated several projects at different stages (see [§6.1](#61-bell-ai-fellowship-spinouts)), and one Fellow got a new AI-related job as a direct result of the program. Over the last thirty years I've incubated, advised, founded, funded, or served on the board of more than 100 technology companies — from raw pre-funding teams to large public companies — spanning hardware/deep-tech infrastructure to consumer apps, plus a handful of non-profit and political organizations. I've also written long-form tech reports on climate tech and nano/micro-tech in the past.

**Why now, specifically:** AI is moving faster than any technology shift I've lived through. Economic power is concentrating. Work is being redefined. The planet is visibly changing. At the same time, young people are capable, adaptable, and thoughtful in ways that give real hope — this isn't a doom framing, it's a responsibility-to-engage framing. The industry itself is unusually unsettled right now: within the same week in August 2026, Google restructured Google DeepMind's leadership (Hassabis stepping back as CEO; Jeff Dean and other long-tenured research leaders departing, some to start their own ventures), and OpenAI and Anthropic have been visibly jockeying on public positioning and policy. None of that is a reason to pick a direction reactively — but it is evidence that the frontier labs are not the only place, or even an obviously stable place, to make a long-term bet from.

## 2. Frameworks for Deciding

Three lenses, used together rather than any one alone:

- **Legacy Lens** — What would future generations, people I will never meet, be grateful I worked on?
- **Historical / Technological Lens** — How does this AI-driven transition compare to past technology revolutions, and what does that comparison predict?
- **Problem-First Lens** — What problems and opportunities consistently pull at my attention? What am I curious about? What might hold my attention for years, not months?

## 3. Decision Criteria

**What resonates tends to share these characteristics:**

- Something I'm uniquely positioned to do, or can position myself to do.
- Something that creates leverage through institutions (existing or new), technology, and great talent — especially the combination of all three.
- Something that can survive beyond my own involvement.
- Fun and joy: real time in the creative act, and room to stay deeply curious.

**What to avoid — cautions against real and perceived weaknesses:**

- Being busy without consequence or direction ("portfolio without purpose").
- Overindexing on monetary outcomes, what's fashionable, or social status.
- Saying "yes" too fast — while also not being decisive when it counts.

**A working constraint:** ideally, the next chapter runs on something close to an academic schedule — intensity from mid-January to May and September to early December. Not ready to go "996" (9am–9pm, 6 days a week). This is the ideal, and it could change given the right circumstances.

## 4. Problems Worth Solving

Career to date has focused on internet, mobile, and climate technologies — bandwidth/content access, email trustworthiness, mobility, EV affordability — plus non-profit work on the climate crisis and electing responsible politicians. The problems below are what's pulling at attention now.

### 4.1 Loneliness & Civic Institutions

The global sense of belonging, identity, and connection is at a low point in my lifetime, and the statistics back it up. Contributing causes include "technologies of distancing" (highways, telephones, television, internet, social media) and the decline of social-connecting institutions (see *Bowling Alone*), reinforced by an ideology that celebrates the individual over the collective.

Technology has acted as a magnifying glass on the self rather than the collective: the **personal** computer, the **I**Phone, the quantified **self**, and AI **personalization** have each, in their own era, reinforced atomization rather than connection.

Many AI leaders propose companion agents as a solution to loneliness. I think this is a vapid and dangerous direction — a bot relationship is not a substitute for a real human relationship, and it's likely to be attractive precisely *because* it's free of the uncomfortable parts of dealing with a real person. The more promising hunch: AI as a coordinator and connector — helping friends find ways to actually meet, surfacing shared events, something like a "quantified community" instead of a quantified self.

There may also be room for new civic institutions, in the spirit of the wave of institution-building around the last comparable period of economic dislocation and wealth disparity (the Rotary Club, Scouting, at the turn of the 19th century). Worth exploring: whether there's appetite for something inspired by Alcoholics Anonymous, the Boy Scouts, and the Rotary Club, updated for modern values.

### 4.2 Trust in Institutions & Information

The fragmentation of the information ecosystem closely tracks the decline in institutional trust. The institutions whose job was to be authoritative about information have lost that authority — arguably an institution's credibility *is* whether its information can be trusted. We're in a cynical moment: "nothing can be trusted," "do your own research" (useful up to a point, corrosive past it), and a technology/economics/politics system that doesn't reward truth-seeking, social connection, or the things that make life worth living.

Past ideas considered: prizes to incentivize truth-telling; a journalism model tied to prediction rather than advertising. What feels newly urgent: as more traffic shifts to AI answer engines, "ground truth" needs some way to connect to the models people actually rely on. Two hunches:

1. Correctness may correlate across domains — a model with correct facts in one category may reason better in others (and the inverse has been shown: training on incorrect information degrades outputs elsewhere).
2. AI-mediated answers may end the open internet as we know it. The incentive to publish openly and indexably weakens once most traffic goes through AI engines that need only a few authoritative sources — which is part of why social platforms feel advantaged (X, YouTube, Facebook) and why OpenAI is building its own social product. A "fenced-in" content internet changes the business model for producing reliable information; citizen-generated eyewitness content (video, audio, text) stays valuable, but funding more rigorous reporting will need a sturdier model than today's social-media economics. One unconventional idea: tie journalism, or whatever it becomes, to prediction markets and the economic value of accurate forecasting.

### 4.3 Education

Higher education has been on a collision course with economic reality for decades — cost is very high, and its value proposition for most people is no longer clear. AI simultaneously accelerates the pace and variety of possible education *and* changes what people actually need to know to succeed in work, citizenship, family, and self-care. This feels like a large problem area; the open question is whether there's something differentiated to contribute.

One live idea (developed with Tenni Theurer, co-founder of the Bell AI Fellowship and former VP of Product at Yahoo): coursework for people who are already AI-curious, aimed at making them dramatically more productive. One version: take real company projects, break them into learnable tasks, have learners execute them, then reintegrate the output into something the company can use — an "externship" rather than an internship. A simpler version: charge for a fast, ROI-focused path to building agents and other immediately useful skills. The bigger vision is still unclear, but the underlying claim is that post-secondary education isn't delivering the right tools at the right price. See [§6.2](#62-ai-dojo--ai-innovation-lab) and [§6.3](#63-the-great-questions-of-artificial-intelligence-seminar) for two concrete, differently-shaped bets already in motion.

### 4.4 Innovation (Acceleration & Deceleration)

A long-standing interest: can a given technology's pace be deliberately accelerated, decelerated, or stopped — and if so, how? This is arguably one of the central questions animating the AI debate, and it isn't new; society has faced versions of it with other technologies. See [§5.3](#53-investigate-innovation-theory) for how this might turn into a body of work rather than a standing interest.

### 4.5 Scaling AI & Energy

The demand for compute scaling is effectively insatiable, and it's currently being met primarily with fossil fuel. Open question: is there a differentiated role in helping accelerate power availability while steering it toward more energy-efficient, lower-carbon sources — rather than treating scaling and decarbonization as opposed.

## 5. Possible Focus Areas

### 5.1 Advise / Mentor / Board Companies

The lowest-friction option. Could mean: expanding advisory involvement at current portfolio companies (several already AI or AI-adjacent); taking on new advisory or board roles (a plausible mix might be a small number of public companies, several private companies, and one non-profit board — roughly eight deep-engagement roles feels like the ceiling, with room for many more lighter-touch ones); or shifting toward **acceleration** — the Bell AI Fellowship surfaced how hard it is to find founders, and high-performing accelerator models exist (YC, A16Z's Speedrun) that the Fellowship could pivot toward joining or resembling, rather than running as an incubator.

A harder but more appealing version is **incubation**: staying closer to a community of founders who are ready to go "996" and hungry to partner with experienced operators. Appealing because it engages curiosity and creativity directly; not yet clear how to make it happen.

### 5.2 Accelerate or Incubate Startups

(See above — kept distinct because "advise existing companies" and "build a pipeline of new ones" are different operating models with different time commitments and different tests of whether they're working.)

### 5.3 Investigate Innovation Theory

A long-running fascination: what actually causes innovation to speed up or slow down. Enough decades of pattern-watching to suspect there's a more structured — maybe eventually a theoretical — account here. Unclear how to proceed, but candidate methods: deliberately compare cases of speed-up and slow-down across companies and technologies; use a podcast or other recurring media as a forcing function and community-builder; let the thinking crystallize the way Clay Christensen's reportedly did on the way to *The Innovator's Dilemma* — not from a tidy starting outline, but from grinding on the material until the structure emerged. A forcing function (teaching a class, writing a book, a lecture series) would likely help. Candidate six-week sprint: a podcast case-study pilot, or committing to six weeks of regular writing/production.

### 5.4 Improve or Create New Education

Two different bets, not one:

- **Improve existing institutions** — lower risk in concept, but carries real reservations about bureaucratic drag. A possible middle path: build shareable examples/models rather than owning implementation, since implementation is where bureaucracy usually wins.
- **Create new structures** — the more ambitious version, asking whether there's a genuinely new way to educate people after secondary school, at scale, for an AI-saturated world. Current education (elementary/secondary especially) is shaped by industrial-era standardization; higher ed by older monastic/university models. The "infinite tutor" enabled by AI might be a first hint of something structurally new rather than an incremental fix — if the AI shift is closer to the industrial revolution than to the internet revolution, the corresponding institutions may need to be built, not retrofitted.

## 6. Active Experiments

These aren't hypothetical focus areas — they're already running, and each one is effectively a live test of one of the directions above.

### 6.1 Bell AI Fellowship Spinouts

Projects and companies that resulted from the Bell AI Fellowship:

- **Storyline** — scalable storytelling that converts voice interviews into compelling video. Status: raising a pre-seed. Needs: funders, a technology co-founder.
- **Flappable** (a "vibe coder" for building voice agents) — status: developer iterating, looking for design partners. Needs: real economically-valuable projects, a business co-founder.
- **Expert OS** — an agent concept, earlier-stage.

### 6.2 AI Dojo / AI Innovation Lab

A six-week, application-only, paid cohort program co-founded with Tenni Theurer (former VP of Product at Yahoo, co-founder of the Bell AI Fellowship), aimed at people who already understand AI matters and want real fluency in their own domain. Structure: weekly two-hour sessions (piloted for Wednesday evenings, in-person kickoff and demo day), curated admissions favoring people with both high need and high capability, so the cohort keeps learning together after the program ends. This is a direct, near-term test of the "improve individual AI fluency at a premium price point" version of the education focus area ([§4.3](#43-education), [§5.4](#54-improve-or-create-new-education)).

### 6.3 *The Great Questions of Artificial Intelligence* (seminar)

A different bet on education: a great-books-style seminar, not a tools/skills course. Small groups (10–15 people), two hours weekly for 13–14 weeks, no lectures and no "expert" — the text is the authority, a tutor keeps discussion on track without imposing opinion (modeled on the St John's College approach). Reading list spans Turing, Hobbes, Descartes, Lovelace, Gödel, Rosenblatt, Rumelhart/Hinton/Williams, Herbert Simon, Searle, Dennett, Nagel, Dreyfus, Varela/Thompson/Rosch, *Attention Is All You Need*, Sutton's *The Bitter Lesson*, the GPT-3 paper, *On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots*, Clark & Chalmers' *The Extended Mind*, and Hannah Arendt's *The Human Condition*. The goal isn't AI fluency — it's using AI as a lens to re-open old questions about intelligence, thinking, understanding, embodiment, and what a society should do with abundant machine intelligence. This tests a very different appeal than the AI Dojo: depth and inquiry over speed and productivity.

## 7. Potential Sprints

Bounded, time-boxed tests designed to convert "this appeals to me" into a real answer — not multi-year commitments.

**Education** — Appeal: feels genuinely broken for the AI era, and an academic schedule is attractive. Worry: is there something both scalable and worth building? Candidate sprints: prototype a year of "mandatory" post-high-school service, starting with a six-week exploration or even a one-month pilot; launch an "AI Dojo"-style paid training pilot.

**Loneliness** — Appeal: civic life is falling apart in ways that threaten wellbeing and democracy. Worry: is there something useful to contribute here specifically, will anyone show up, and will it turn into 996 if it works? Candidate sprints: prototype a new civic institution built around face-to-face interaction; prototype an app that facilitates it.

**AI and Democracy** — Appeal: the moment feels ripe to put ideas and tools into practice. Worry: can momentum in ideas, funding, and people actually be built? Candidate sprints: assemble a salon on the topic; explore what a "national public LLM" would even mean.

**Innovation Theory** — Appeal: thinking and building a platform to engage a topic of deep, durable curiosity. Worry: can the quiet, contemplative pace be tolerated, and can the content actually get produced? Candidate sprints: a podcast case study; a six-week commitment to regular writing or production.

## 8. Open Questions & Next Steps

- **Narrow the list.** The near-term goal is a shorter list of directions, specifically so real career sprints can start — not more surface area.
- **Which sprint first?** Education has two live vehicles already running (AI Dojo, the seminar); loneliness/civic work has appeal but no pilot yet. Does a first move toward loneliness/civic institutions make sense now, or does it wait until one of the education experiments produces a clear signal?
- **What does the current industry churn actually imply?** Leadership instability at Google DeepMind and public positioning battles between OpenAI and Anthropic are evidence the frontier is unsettled — worth periodically asking whether that changes the calculus on any focus area above (e.g., does it make "advise/board" roles at frontier labs more or less attractive; does it open a specific opening for independent, cross-lab convening work).
- **Test the "portfolio without purpose" failure mode directly.** Given how many appealing directions are listed here, the sharpest immediate risk named in [§3](#3-decision-criteria) is exactly this document turning into one more open-ended portfolio. Worth revisiting on a fixed cadence (quarterly?) with a simple test: did a sprint actually conclude with a decision, or just extend?
