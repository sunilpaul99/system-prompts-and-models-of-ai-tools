## 7. The Council Chamber: How AI Politics Arrives in Local Government

**One-line pitch.** National AI politics gets all the attention, but AI is actually entering
American governance through tens of thousands of city councils and school boards — and their
deliberations are public, transcribed, and almost completely unstudied.

**Motivation.** Our polarization-index pitch (idea 2) watches elites in Washington; but most
citizens will first encounter AI *as a political question* locally — a school board debating
classroom chatbot policy, a city council voting on police use of facial recognition or
AI-drafted staff reports, a county weighing a data-center tax break against water use. Local
government is where national ideology meets lived consequence, and the policy-diffusion
literature (how innovations spread city-to-city) has never had a technology arrive this fast.
The data are extraordinary and untapped: Legistar/Granicus agendas and minutes, YouTube
captions of council and school-board meetings, and local news archives cover thousands of
jurisdictions with full text.

**Research questions.**
1. Diffusion: when does "AI" first appear on local agendas, on which issues (education, policing,
   data centers, procurement, workforce), and does adoption spread through the classic channels —
   neighboring jurisdictions, league-of-cities networks, model ordinances from advocacy groups?
2. Framing: do local deliberations use the national debate's vocabulary (safety, innovation,
   bias) or a distinct local one (jobs, water, kids, budgets)? This directly tests our
   translation-gap thesis at the level of government closest to voters.
3. Conflict: which AI issues actually produce contested votes and public-comment mobilization
   locally, versus passing on consent calendars — i.e., where is the real grassroots heat?

**Method and data.** Build a corpus from public Legistar/Granicus portals (hundreds of cities
expose full agendas/minutes with APIs), YouTube auto-captions of council and school-board
meetings, and Ballotpedia/local news for outcomes. Keyword-seeded then classifier-refined
detection of AI agenda items; event-history models for diffusion; frame analysis on
deliberation text; public-comment volume as the mobilization measure.

**Deliverable.** A paper for *Urban Affairs Review* or *Policy Studies Journal*, plus a public
"AI in Local Government Tracker" — a map that journalists, advocates, and model-ordinance
drafters would use constantly. Nobody has built it.

**Feasibility and risks.** Medium-high. The corpus assembly is the real work — portals are
heterogeneous (mitigate: start with the ~300 largest cities on Legistar's consistent API, expand
later). Caption quality varies (mitigate: agendas/minutes are clean text; captions are
supplementary). No ethics concerns: these are public officials in public meetings.

**Why us.** It grounds our whole program: the ideologies map and polarization index describe
the air war; this is the ground war, measured for the first time.

## 8. Who Called It? An Accountability Audit of a Decade of AI Predictions

**One-line pitch.** Thousands of confident public predictions about AI — from experts, pundits,
executives, and prediction markets — have now quietly resolved; score them all, Tetlock-style,
and establish who has actually earned epistemic authority in the AI debate.

**Motivation.** The AI discourse runs on borrowed credibility: "godfathers of AI," lab CEOs,
celebrity skeptics, and forecasting platforms all make claims about what's coming, and almost
nobody goes back to check. Yet the receipts are public: AI Impacts' expert surveys (2016, 2022,
2023) recorded median timelines for specific capabilities; Metaculus and Manifold questions
have resolved by the hundreds; pundits' predictions sit timestamped in op-eds, podcasts, and
tweets preserved by the Wayback Machine. Tetlock showed political experts barely beat chance
and that accountability changes behavior; nobody has run the exercise for AI, the domain where
forecast quality now matters most. Our prior study found expert timelines collapsed 13 years in
one year — was that update toward accuracy, or herding?

**Research questions.**
1. Calibration: scoring only pre-registered, resolvable claims, how accurate were (a) surveyed
   AI researchers, (b) prediction-market crowds, (c) prominent public commentators, and
   (d) lab leadership — and who wins?
2. Signatures: do over- and under-prediction cluster by ideology (accelerationist vs. skeptic
   vs. safety-focused), by proximity to the technology, or by incentive (fundraising,
   book-selling)?
3. Market efficiency: did prediction markets anticipate expert-survey updates, or lag them —
   i.e., where should a rational observer actually look for AI foresight?

**Method and data.** Assemble a claims database: AI Impacts / ESPAI survey items with resolution
criteria; resolved Metaculus/Manifold AI questions (public archives); a systematically sampled
pundit corpus (op-eds, transcripts, timestamped via Wayback) restricted to operationalizable
claims, with blind independent resolution coding to avoid cherry-picking. Score with Brier
scores and calibration curves; compare cohorts.

**Deliverable.** A paper (*PNAS Nexus*, or the forecasting literature's home journals) plus the
public artifact with teeth: a maintained "AI Prediction Scorecard." High press interest is
guaranteed, which is exactly the accountability mechanism Tetlock says improves discourse.

**Feasibility and risks.** Medium. The central threat is selection bias in which pundit claims
get scored (mitigate: pre-registered sampling frame — e.g., every AI prediction in the top-20
op-ed pages 2015–2024 — and blind resolution by coders who don't know the claimant). Resolution
ambiguity is real ("AGI by X" is unscoreable; "self-driving taxis in 10 cities" is not) —
score only operationalizable claims and report the exclusion rate honestly.

**Feasibility bonus:** the hardest part — finding old predictions — is exactly what our
digital-trace methods are good at.

**Why us.** Our ideologies map profiles the belief communities; this study tests which of them
can actually see the future — the missing empirical column in that map.

## 9. The Telephone Game: How AI Incident Stories Mutate as They Spread

**One-line pitch.** A handful of canonical AI mishap stories — the lawyer's fake citations, the
airline chatbot's invented refund policy, the "drone that killed its operator" — structure
public fear of AI, and each mutated measurably as it spread; trace the full lineage of every
major AI legend for the first time.

**Motivation.** Classic rumor research (Allport & Postman's serial reproduction experiments)
showed stories systematically sharpen, level, and assimilate to prior beliefs as they pass
between people — but it could only study transmission in the lab. AI incidents give us the
in-the-wild version with complete fossil records: the "AI drone kills operator" story went from
a hypothetical thought experiment at a conference to a reported "simulation" to a viral "an AI
killed its operator" across millions of impressions, with every retelling timestamped and
linkable. These legends matter: they are the concrete anecdotes through which mass publics —
and legislators, who cite them in hearings — reason about AI risk. Nobody has mapped how
faithfully the load-bearing stories of AI politics track their sources.

**Research questions.**
1. Mutation dynamics: across the 20–30 most-cited AI incidents (sourced from the AI Incident
   Database plus congressional-hearing citations), how do key facts drift as stories move
   between platforms and outlets — and do distortions follow Allport & Postman's sharpening
   and assimilation patterns?
2. Direction of drift: do mutations systematically inflate agency ("the AI decided...") and
   severity, and does drift direction depend on the sharing community's prior (per our
   ideologies map: does the same story mutate differently through doomer, accelerationist,
   and mainstream channels)?
3. Correction dynamics: when the original source issues a correction (as in the drone case),
   how far does the correction propagate relative to the mutation?

**Method and data.** For each incident: identify the origin document (court filing, tribunal
ruling, conference talk, incident report — all public); collect the retelling cascade via news
archives (GDELT, MediaCloud), social platforms, and YouTube transcripts; extract claim
tuples (who acted, what happened, harm, intent) from each retelling with LLM-assisted
extraction and hand validation; measure fidelity-to-source over hops and time; compare
correction cascades to mutation cascades.

**Deliverable.** A paper for *Nature Human Behaviour* or the misinformation literature
(*HKS Misinformation Review* for speed), plus a compelling public artifact: interactive
"genealogies" of the great AI legends showing exactly where each false detail entered.

**Feasibility and risks.** Medium-high. Cascade reconstruction is incomplete off any single
platform (mitigate: news-archive backbone plus platform samples; report coverage). Claim
extraction at scale needs validation (mitigate: hand-coded gold subset). The incident list must
avoid selection toward juicy mutations (mitigate: fix the sample by citation frequency in the
AI Incident Database and congressional records *before* measuring drift).

**Why us.** It fuses our two proven competences — mapping AI belief communities and tracing
public digital records — into the study of the stories those communities actually run on.
