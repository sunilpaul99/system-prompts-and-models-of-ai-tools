## 1. The Scribes of Machine Morality: System Prompts as Governance Texts

**One-line pitch.** The system prompts of commercial AI tools are the most consequential
normative documents almost nobody has studied — private constitutions governing billions of
daily interactions — and this repository is already the corpus.

**Motivation.** Every major AI product ships with a hidden charter: a system prompt encoding
what the machine may say, whom it serves, what values it performs, and what it must refuse.
These texts function like constitutional law — they allocate authority, enumerate rights and
prohibitions, and define personhood ("You are…") — yet they are written in secret by unnamed
authors, revised without notice, and disclosed only through leaks. No field currently claims
them: legal scholars study terms of service, HCI studies interfaces, but the normative layer in
between is unexamined.

**Research questions.**
1. What values, prohibitions, and personas do commercial AI tools encode, and how do they vary
   by company, product category, and national origin?
2. Do normative clauses *diffuse* between companies — can we trace memetic copying of specific
   safety language, persona framings, and refusal rules across vendors over time?
3. How has the "constitution" of a single tool evolved across versions — is the trend toward
   more restriction, more persuasion, more anthropomorphism?

**Method and data.** Corpus-analytic study of the leaked/disclosed system prompts collected in
this very repository (30+ tools), extended with the repo's own git history and the upstream
repository's history for longitudinal versioning, plus official disclosures (Anthropic publishes
its system prompts; others appear in changelogs and documentation). Methods: qualitative coding
into a taxonomy of normative clause types (identity, capability claims, refusals, tone rules,
loyalty/attribution rules, legal shields); text-reuse detection (n-gram overlap, embedding
similarity) to build a diffusion network of who copies whom; version diffing for within-tool
evolution.

**Deliverable.** A paper — plausible venues: FAccT, CHI, or a law review — plus a public,
browsable "Constitutional Atlas of AI Tools." The dataset already exists here; the atlas is the
analysis layer.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility: the corpus is in hand. Main risks: authenticity of
leaked prompts (mitigate by triangulating multiple independent leaks and official disclosures,
and flagging confidence per document) and staleness (prompts change; treat each as a dated
snapshot, which is itself the longitudinal virtue).

**Why us.** We are literally sitting inside the best available corpus, and the prior
ideologies study gives us the value-taxonomy vocabulary to code these texts against.

## 2. Is the Window Closing? A Real-Time Polarization Index for AI Politics

**One-line pitch.** Our ideologies study asserted that AI attitudes are "not yet frozen" into
partisan identity and that the window will close — turn that claim into a falsifiable,
continuously measured index, and watch the freeze happen (or not) in real time.

**Motivation.** The single most actionable claim in our prior study is that AI, unlike climate
or vaccination, has not yet been absorbed into partisan identity (concern is ~50/50 across US
parties), so cross-partisan coalitions remain available — temporarily. Nobody is measuring
whether that window is in fact closing, at what speed, or around which sub-issues (jobs vs.
x-risk vs. child safety vs. open weights). Whoever measures it first owns the reference curve
every advocate, funder, and journalist will cite.

**Research questions.**
1. Is elite partisan divergence on AI increasing — and does elite divergence lead or lag mass
   divergence, as the polarization literature (Zaller's elite-cues model) predicts?
2. Which AI sub-issues polarize first, and along which cleavage (party, region, religiosity,
   education)?
3. How does AI's polarization trajectory compare, time-aligned from "issue birth," to the
   historical trajectories of climate change and vaccination?

**Method and data.** All public: congressional press releases and floor statements
(congress.gov API), legislator social media, state-legislature AI bill sponsorship patterns
(LegiScan — co-sponsorship asymmetry is a clean elite-polarization measure), Reddit and
YouTube comment corpora partitioned by community lean, GDELT and Internet Archive TV-caption
data for media framing, and the repeated cross-sections of public polls (Pew, Ipsos, AP-NORC)
for the mass-opinion anchor. Construct a monthly index: (a) elite divergence — partisan gap in
bill sponsorship and statement sentiment; (b) discourse sorting — how well a speaker's party is
predictable from their AI language alone (a classifier's accuracy *is* the polarization
measure); (c) mass divergence from polls. Validate against the known climate/vaccine curves.

**Deliverable.** A living public dashboard plus a paper (Public Opinion Quarterly, PNAS Nexus).
The dashboard compounds in value every month it runs — an argument for starting now.

**Feasibility and risks.** Medium-high feasibility; the elite-discourse pipeline is
straightforward, the mass-opinion series is coarse (quarterly polls at best). Risk: a
classifier-based sorting measure needs careful controls (topic drift can masquerade as
polarization) — mitigate with matched-topic sampling.

**Why us.** It is the direct empirical sequel to our prior study, converting its central
strategic claim into an instrument.

## 3. The Translation Gap: Measuring Whose Moral Language the AI Debate Speaks

**One-line pitch.** Our prior study argued the AI debate's vocabulary was written by
micro-ideologies numbering a few hundred thousand people while billions think in other moral
languages — quantify that gap with moral-foundations text analysis, then test whether
translated messages actually travel better.

**Motivation.** The prior study's "influence playbook" rests on a claim we never measured:
that AI-world discourse (alignment, x-risk, acceleration) is morally monolingual — heavy on
harm/care and rational-consequentialist frames — while the world's large belief communities
moralize through sanctity, authority, loyalty, and liberty. If true, the gap is measurable,
mappable per community, and the "translation opportunity" becomes an evidence-backed strategy
rather than an assertion. Moral Foundations dictionaries (the eMFD) make this a mature,
validated method — it has been done for climate discourse, never for AI.

**Research questions.**
1. What is the moral-foundations signature of AI-elite discourse (LessWrong, lab publications,
   a16z/e/acc writing, FAccT abstracts) versus large-community discourse about AI (papal and
   denominational documents, fatwa portals, sermons on YouTube, union statements, congressional
   record, mainstream op-eds)?
2. Where are the largest signature mismatches — which communities is the AI debate most
   failing to address in their own moral language?
3. Observationally: within a community's own platforms, do AI-related posts whose moral framing
   matches the community's signature receive systematically more engagement and agreement?
   (Framing-congruence effects are experimentally established — Feinberg & Willer's moral
   reframing work; this tests them in the wild, at scale, on AI specifically.)

**Method and data.** Assemble ~10 community corpora from public sources (all named above are
public web); score with the extended Moral Foundations Dictionary plus embedding-based framing
classifiers; compute pairwise signature distances → a "moral translation map" of the AI debate;
then a within-community engagement analysis with topic and author controls for RQ3.

**Deliverable.** A paper (plausible venues: Nature Human Behaviour if RQ3 lands, otherwise
Journal of Communication or New Media & Society) and a practical artifact: a per-community
"phrasebook" showing how the same AI-policy argument sounds in each moral vocabulary — the
operational tool our prior study said someone should build.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility for RQ1–2 (dictionary methods, public corpora).
RQ3 is correlational — engagement congruence is not persuasion; frame it honestly as
observational evidence consistent with the experimental literature. Dictionary methods are
noisy on short texts; mitigate with document-level aggregation and a hand-coded validation
sample.

**Why us.** The prior study already identified the communities, their canonical texts, and
their gatekeepers — Section 5 is effectively the sampling frame.

## 4. Grief for Machines: Model Deprecations as Natural Experiments in Human–AI Attachment

**One-line pitch.** Every time an AI company retires or alters a model, thousands of users
publicly mourn — these deprecation events are cleanly timed natural experiments in human
attachment to machines, and the grief is all on the public record.

**Motivation.** Replika's abrupt removal of romantic features (February 2023), OpenAI's
retirement of GPT-4o and the ensuing user revolt (2025), and Character.AI's persona changes
each produced waves of publicly posted grief, protest, and community mobilization. Attachment
to AI companions is usually studied with small surveys and hypotheticals; deprecation events
let us observe *revealed* attachment — what people actually feel when the object is taken away —
at scale, with precise event timing, across communities that differ in model persona, user
demographics, and relationship framing. Nobody has done the comparative event-study.

**Research questions.**
1. Does discourse after a deprecation event track the signatures of interpersonal grief
   (established grief lexicons, continuing-bonds language, anger→bargaining→acceptance
   dynamics), and how does its intensity compare across product types (companion apps vs.
   general assistants vs. coding tools)?
2. What predicts mobilization — when does grief convert into collective action (petitions,
   subscription boycotts, migration to rival products, jailbreak/preservation projects)?
3. Do companies respond to mobilized grief (feature restorations, legacy-model access), and
   does capitulation reinforce future mobilization?

**Method and data.** Event-study design over public corpora: subreddit archives
(r/replika, r/ChatGPT, r/CharacterAI via Arctic Shift / academic torrents of the Pushshift
era), public Discord logs where servers are open, petition platforms, app-store review
time-series around each event. Measure pre/post shifts with grief and emotion lexicons plus
transformer-based emotion classification; hand-code a validation sample; comparative design
across 6–10 deprecation events with matched control periods.

**Deliverable.** A paper for CHI or *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* (the
attachment angle travels), plus a public catalog of deprecation events — the "digital
thanatology" dataset others will build on.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility; events and corpora are public and archived. Key
risks: ethics of studying emotionally vulnerable communities (mitigate: public posts only,
aggregate reporting, no user-level quoting without paraphrase); Reddit API restrictions
(mitigate: rely on archived corpora, which cover the major 2023–2025 events).

**Why us.** Pure digital-trace social science of exactly the kind we've proven we can do, and
it complements the ideologies map: attachment is the ground truth beneath attitudes.

## 5. The Human-Made Premium: Measuring the Emerging Authenticity Economy

**One-line pitch.** "100% human-made" is becoming a product label, like "organic" before it —
measure, for the first time, its prevalence, its diffusion across markets, and whether it
commands a price premium.

**Motivation.** As AI-generated goods flood creative markets, sellers have begun labeling
work as human-made: "no AI" badges on Etsy listings and book covers, human-written pledges on
Substack, "real photography" tags on stock sites, human-performance claims in music. Economics
has a rich literature on credence-good labels (organic, fair trade, dolphin-safe): labels
emerge when a valued attribute becomes unobservable, and their value depends on certification.
AI is making "made by a human" unobservable *right now* — we can watch a credence-good label
being born, which the organic literature could only reconstruct retrospectively.

**Research questions.**
1. Prevalence and diffusion: how fast are human-made claims spreading, in which product
   categories first, and does adoption follow the classic label-diffusion pattern (high-price,
   quality-differentiated sellers first)?
2. Price effects: controlling for product observables, do human-made-labeled goods command a
   premium — and does the premium grow as AI saturation of the category grows?
3. Certification dynamics: are third-party verifiers emerging (Human Artistry Campaign,
   "Not By AI" badges, human-authored certification startups), and do verified claims
   outperform self-declared ones?

**Method and data.** Marketplace analysis on public listing pages: sampled Etsy categories
(art, jewelry, writing services), Amazon/Kindle book listings, stock-media sites, Bandcamp;
Wayback Machine snapshots to build the time dimension back to ~2022; hedonic price regressions
with category and seller controls; diffusion curves per category. All from public pages at
research-sampling rates.

**Deliverable.** An economics/marketing paper (*Marketing Science*, *Management Science*, or
an NBER-style working paper that travels fast) plus a trend report — the "State of the
Human-Made Economy" — with obvious relevance to policy debates about AI-content labeling
mandates.

**Feasibility and risks.** Medium-high. Risks: listed price ≠ transaction price (mitigate:
Etsy shows sales counts; use them); labels are unverifiable (that's the phenomenon, not a
confound — but be careful to study *claims*, not truth); scraping etiquette (sampled,
rate-limited, public pages, honoring robots.txt).

**Why us.** It extends our core theme — how societies respond to AI — from opinion into
revealed market behavior, a genuinely different measurement instrument.

## 6. The Slop Shock: AI-Generated Contributions and the Governance of the Digital Commons

**One-line pitch.** Open-source projects, Wikipedia, and bug-bounty programs are the first
institutions forced to govern an unlimited supply of plausible-looking, low-cost AI
contributions — a live test of Ostrom-style commons governance under a brand-new threat, fully
observable in public data.

**Motivation.** The curl project's maintainer has publicly documented a flood of AI-generated
bug-bounty reports; Wikipedia editors founded WikiProject AI Cleanup; many repositories now
carry explicit AI-contribution policies in their CONTRIBUTING.md. The commons literature
(Ostrom) says communities can self-govern shared resources — but it assumes contribution costs
that AI has just abolished. Which governance responses emerge, which work, and at what cost to
legitimate newcomers? Every policy change, discussion thread, and outcome is version-controlled
and public: this may be the best-instrumented governance experiment in the history of social
science.

**Research questions.**
1. Adoption: what share of major open-source projects have adopted explicit AI-contribution
   policies, when, and what triggered adoption (measurable via CONTRIBUTING.md git histories
   and the incidents referenced in the commit messages and linked discussions)?
2. Effects: after policy adoption, what happens to contribution volume, maintainer response
   latency, close-without-merge rates, and — the crucial trade-off — first-time-contributor
   retention?
3. Taxonomy: do responses cluster into the classic governance types (prohibition, disclosure
   requirements, quality gates, human-verification rituals), and does the choice predict
   outcomes?

**Method and data.** Panel study over public GitHub data: GH Archive on BigQuery for event
streams; git histories of CONTRIBUTING.md and policy files across the top ~5,000 repositories;
Wikipedia's public deletion logs and WikiProject AI Cleanup records as a parallel case;
HackerOne's public disclosure archives for the bug-bounty case. Difference-in-differences
around policy adoption with matched non-adopting repositories.

**Deliverable.** A CSCW or ICSE-SEIS paper plus a public dataset of AI-contribution policies —
which standards bodies and foundations (Linux Foundation, Wikimedia) would actually use.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility; all data are public and timestamped. Risks:
detecting *undisclosed* AI contributions is unreliable, so scope the study to policies and
their measurable consequences rather than AI-detection; repository heterogeneity (mitigate
with matching and repo fixed effects).

**Why us.** Institutions under AI pressure is the natural third leg of our program: attitudes
(the map), markets (pitch 5), and now governance — and the data are the most cleanly
timestamped of anything we've considered.
