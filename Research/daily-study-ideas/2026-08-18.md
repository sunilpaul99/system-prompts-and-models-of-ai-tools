# Daily Study Ideas — August 18, 2026

Three pitches, each feasible with entirely public digital sources.

## 1. The Scribes of Machine Morality: System Prompts as Governance Texts

**One-line pitch.** The system prompts of commercial AI tools are the most consequential
normative documents almost nobody has studied — private constitutions governing billions of
daily interactions — and this repository is already the corpus.

**Motivation.** Every major AI product ships with a hidden charter: a system prompt encoding
what the machine may say, whom it serves, what values it performs, and what it must refuse.
These texts function like constitutional law — they allocate authority, enumerate rights and
prohibitions, and define personhood ("You are…") — yet they are written in secret by unnamed
authors, revised without notice, and disclosed only through leaks. No field currently claims
them: legal scholars study terms of service, HCI studies interfaces, but the normative layer in
between is unexamined.

**Research questions.**
1. What values, prohibitions, and personas do commercial AI tools encode, and how do they vary
   by company, product category, and national origin?
2. Do normative clauses *diffuse* between companies — can we trace memetic copying of specific
   safety language, persona framings, and refusal rules across vendors over time?
3. How has the "constitution" of a single tool evolved across versions — is the trend toward
   more restriction, more persuasion, more anthropomorphism?

**Method and data.** Corpus-analytic study of the leaked/disclosed system prompts collected in
this very repository (30+ tools), extended with the repo's own git history and the upstream
repository's history for longitudinal versioning, plus official disclosures (Anthropic publishes
its system prompts; others appear in changelogs and documentation). Methods: qualitative coding
into a taxonomy of normative clause types (identity, capability claims, refusals, tone rules,
loyalty/attribution rules, legal shields); text-reuse detection (n-gram overlap, embedding
similarity) to build a diffusion network of who copies whom; version diffing for within-tool
evolution.

**Deliverable.** A paper — plausible venues: FAccT, CHI, or a law review — plus a public,
browsable "Constitutional Atlas of AI Tools." The dataset already exists here; the atlas is the
analysis layer.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility: the corpus is in hand. Main risks: authenticity of
leaked prompts (mitigate by triangulating multiple independent leaks and official disclosures,
and flagging confidence per document) and staleness (prompts change; treat each as a dated
snapshot, which is itself the longitudinal virtue).

**Why us.** We are literally sitting inside the best available corpus, and the prior
ideologies study gives us the value-taxonomy vocabulary to code these texts against.

## 2. Is the Window Closing? A Real-Time Polarization Index for AI Politics

**One-line pitch.** Our ideologies study asserted that AI attitudes are "not yet frozen" into
partisan identity and that the window will close — turn that claim into a falsifiable,
continuously measured index, and watch the freeze happen (or not) in real time.

**Motivation.** The single most actionable claim in our prior study is that AI, unlike climate
or vaccination, has not yet been absorbed into partisan identity (concern is ~50/50 across US
parties), so cross-partisan coalitions remain available — temporarily. Nobody is measuring
whether that window is in fact closing, at what speed, or around which sub-issues (jobs vs.
x-risk vs. child safety vs. open weights). Whoever measures it first owns the reference curve
every advocate, funder, and journalist will cite.

**Research questions.**
1. Is elite partisan divergence on AI increasing — and does elite divergence lead or lag mass
   divergence, as the polarization literature (Zaller's elite-cues model) predicts?
2. Which AI sub-issues polarize first, and along which cleavage (party, region, religiosity,
   education)?
3. How does AI's polarization trajectory compare, time-aligned from "issue birth," to the
   historical trajectories of climate change and vaccination?

**Method and data.** All public: congressional press releases and floor statements
(congress.gov API), legislator social media, state-legislature AI bill sponsorship patterns
(LegiScan — co-sponsorship asymmetry is a clean elite-polarization measure), Reddit and
YouTube comment corpora partitioned by community lean, GDELT and Internet Archive TV-caption
data for media framing, and the repeated cross-sections of public polls (Pew, Ipsos, AP-NORC)
for the mass-opinion anchor. Construct a monthly index: (a) elite divergence — partisan gap in
bill sponsorship and statement sentiment; (b) discourse sorting — how well a speaker's party is
predictable from their AI language alone (a classifier's accuracy *is* the polarization
measure); (c) mass divergence from polls. Validate against the known climate/vaccine curves.

**Deliverable.** A living public dashboard plus a paper (Public Opinion Quarterly, PNAS Nexus).
The dashboard compounds in value every month it runs — an argument for starting now.

**Feasibility and risks.** Medium-high feasibility; the elite-discourse pipeline is
straightforward, the mass-opinion series is coarse (quarterly polls at best). Risk: a
classifier-based sorting measure needs careful controls (topic drift can masquerade as
polarization) — mitigate with matched-topic sampling.

**Why us.** It is the direct empirical sequel to our prior study, converting its central
strategic claim into an instrument.

## 3. The Translation Gap: Measuring Whose Moral Language the AI Debate Speaks

**One-line pitch.** Our prior study argued the AI debate's vocabulary was written by
micro-ideologies numbering a few hundred thousand people while billions think in other moral
languages — quantify that gap with moral-foundations text analysis, then test whether
translated messages actually travel better.

**Motivation.** The prior study's "influence playbook" rests on a claim we never measured:
that AI-world discourse (alignment, x-risk, acceleration) is morally monolingual — heavy on
harm/care and rational-consequentialist frames — while the world's large belief communities
moralize through sanctity, authority, loyalty, and liberty. If true, the gap is measurable,
mappable per community, and the "translation opportunity" becomes an evidence-backed strategy
rather than an assertion. Moral Foundations dictionaries (the eMFD) make this a mature,
validated method — it has been done for climate discourse, never for AI.

**Research questions.**
1. What is the moral-foundations signature of AI-elite discourse (LessWrong, lab publications,
   a16z/e/acc writing, FAccT abstracts) versus large-community discourse about AI (papal and
   denominational documents, fatwa portals, sermons on YouTube, union statements, congressional
   record, mainstream op-eds)?
2. Where are the largest signature mismatches — which communities is the AI debate most
   failing to address in their own moral language?
3. Observationally: within a community's own platforms, do AI-related posts whose moral framing
   matches the community's signature receive systematically more engagement and agreement?
   (Framing-congruence effects are experimentally established — Feinberg & Willer's moral
   reframing work; this tests them in the wild, at scale, on AI specifically.)

**Method and data.** Assemble ~10 community corpora from public sources (all named above are
public web); score with the extended Moral Foundations Dictionary plus embedding-based framing
classifiers; compute pairwise signature distances → a "moral translation map" of the AI debate;
then a within-community engagement analysis with topic and author controls for RQ3.

**Deliverable.** A paper (plausible venues: Nature Human Behaviour if RQ3 lands, otherwise
Journal of Communication or New Media & Society) and a practical artifact: a per-community
"phrasebook" showing how the same AI-policy argument sounds in each moral vocabulary — the
operational tool our prior study said someone should build.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility for RQ1–2 (dictionary methods, public corpora).
RQ3 is correlational — engagement congruence is not persuasion; frame it honestly as
observational evidence consistent with the experimental literature. Dictionary methods are
noisy on short texts; mitigate with document-level aggregation and a hand-coded validation
sample.

**Why us.** The prior study already identified the communities, their canonical texts, and
their gatekeepers — Section 5 is effectively the sampling frame.
