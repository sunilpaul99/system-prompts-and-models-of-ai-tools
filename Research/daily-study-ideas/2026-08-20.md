Three pitches, each feasible with entirely public digital sources. New fronts today:
comparative law, the knowledge commons, and corporate finance.

## 10. The Legislative Genome: Who Actually Writes AI Law

**One-line pitch.** Sequence-align the text of every AI bill across US states and major
jurisdictions worldwide to expose the copy-paste genealogy of AI law — revealing which model
bills, lobbyists, and regulatory templates are the true, largely invisible authors of AI
governance.

**Motivation.** Hundreds of AI bills have been introduced across US statehouses, and dozens of
countries have drafted AI statutes since the EU AI Act. Legislation is rarely written from
scratch: statehouse scholarship (and the "copy-paste legislation" journalism of the ALEC
investigations) shows bills descend from model texts drafted by advocacy groups, industry, and
transnational bodies. Nobody has built the family tree for AI law — even though the method
(text-reuse detection, the same alignment logic as genomics) is proven and every input is
public. The payoff: an empirical answer to "who governs AI?" that goes beyond who *votes* to
who *drafts* — including a measurement of the "Brussels effect" (EU AI Act language propagating
into other jurisdictions) versus industry counter-drafting.

**Research questions.**
1. Lineage: what fraction of AI-bill text is inherited rather than original, and what are the
   principal ancestral sources (EU AI Act, NIST frameworks, ALEC-style industry models,
   advocacy templates like CCOPS, other states' passed statutes)?
2. Carriers: which organizations' fingerprints (distinctive clause signatures) appear most,
   and do industry-descended bills differ systematically in enforcement teeth (private right
   of action, audit mandates, penalty structure) from advocacy-descended ones?
3. Selection: do inherited bills pass at higher rates than original drafts — is copying the
   price of passage?

**Method and data.** Corpus: LegiScan's public dataset of state AI bills (full text, status,
sponsors); Congress.gov; official texts of the EU AI Act, Council of Europe convention,
Canadian/Brazilian/Korean/Chinese statutes (official translations); known model-bill libraries.
Method: local sequence alignment / n-gram fingerprinting at the clause level (the established
copy-paste-legislation toolkit), producing a directed inheritance graph over bills; hand-coded
enforcement-strength scoring of key clause families; survival analysis of passage rates by
ancestry.

**Deliverable.** A paper for a law review or *Regulation & Governance*, plus a public
interactive "family tree of AI law" — journalists covering any new AI bill would use it to
answer "where did this text come from?" within minutes.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility: texts are public, the method is mature, and the
corpus is a few thousand documents — small by our standards. Risks: translation artifacts for
non-English statutes (mitigate: run cross-language analysis on official English versions only,
clearly scoped); attributing a clause to a source requires the source predating it (mitigate:
strict timestamping; unresolved ancestry reported as such). Complements active study #7
perfectly: #7 tracks local *adoption*, this tracks the *supply chain of text* one level up.

**Why us.** Text-reuse detection over public document corpora is squarely our toolkit, and the
result plugs straight into our influence-mapping program: gatekeepers, but for statutes.

## 11. Where Did the Questions Go? The Great Q&A Migration After LLMs

**One-line pitch.** Humanity ran a twenty-year experiment in public collective knowledge —
Stack Overflow, Stack Exchange, Reddit help communities — and LLMs are quietly ending it;
measure exactly which questions left public space, which remain, and what the disappearance of
public questions costs the commons that trained the models in the first place.

**Motivation.** Stack Overflow's question volume has collapsed since ChatGPT — that headline is
known. What nobody has done is the fine-grained autopsy across the *whole* public Q&A
ecosystem: Stack Exchange publishes complete data dumps for ~180 sites spanning programming,
math, cooking, parenting, philosophy, and Islam/Christianity/Judaism; Reddit help subreddits
are archived; Quora is crawlable. This is a natural experiment in what happens to a knowledge
commons when a private substitute appears: which question *types* migrate first (novice
how-tos vs. expert edge cases), whether answer quality and answerer retention decay for the
questions that remain, and the recursive twist — the public commons that trained LLMs is being
drained by them, which is the data-commons version of eating your seed corn. The heterogeneity
across 180 topic sites is the scientific gold: parenting advice and abstract algebra face the
same shock with very different substitutability.

**Research questions.**
1. Composition: post-LLM, did *routine* questions vanish faster than *novel* ones (measurable
   via duplicate-likelihood, question complexity metrics, and embedding distance from existing
   answers), leaving a harder residual corpus?
2. Heterogeneity: does decline scale with how well LLMs answer the site's domain (proxy:
   benchmark performance by domain; a dose-response design across the 180 sites)?
3. Commons health: what happened to answerer participation, first-answer latency, and new-user
   retention for the questions that stayed — is the commons stabilizing at a smaller expert
   core, or unraveling?
4. The recursion: estimate the shrinkage of fresh public training text in these domains —
   the empirical footing for the "model collapse of the commons" debate.

**Method and data.** Stack Exchange public data dumps (complete post histories, all sites,
through present); Arctic Shift Reddit archives for help subreddits; interrupted time-series
around the ChatGPT release and subsequent capability jumps, with site-level dose-response
(domain substitutability) as the key design; survival models for contributor retention.
All bulk public data — no scraping friction at all.

**Deliverable.** A paper aimed high — *PNAS* or *Nature Human Behaviour* (the commons framing
travels far beyond tech) — plus a public "State of the Knowledge Commons" dashboard by domain.

**Feasibility and risks.** High feasibility; the data dumps make this the least
collection-risky study we've considered. Risks: attributing decline to LLMs vs. secular
platform decay (mitigate: pre-2022 trend controls, and the dose-response design is the real
identification — sites LLMs answer poorly are the comparison group); Stack Exchange policy
changes as confounders (mitigate: event log of platform changes, exclude affected windows).

**Why us.** Bulk digital-trace analysis with a clean natural experiment — our sweet spot — and
it pairs with #6 (slop shock): supply-side pollution there, demand-side drainage here. Together
they're a book-sized statement about AI and the knowledge commons.

## 12. The AI-Washing Index: Corporate AI Talk versus AI Substance

**One-line pitch.** Every public company now says "AI" on earnings calls; build the first
systematic index of the gap between firms' AI *talk* (calls, 10-Ks) and AI *substance*
(hiring, patents, products) — and test whether the gap predicts returns, enforcement, and
quiet abandonment.

**Motivation.** The SEC has already brought AI-washing enforcement actions, and "AI mentions"
on earnings calls have become a market-moving ritual. Finance research measured this for
greenwashing and crypto-washing; AI-washing is bigger and unmeasured at scale. All inputs are
public: every 10-K and earnings-call transcript is in SEC EDGAR filings and IR archives;
hiring substance is observable in public careers pages and archived job postings (Wayback);
patents are public (USPTO/Google Patents); product reality is observable in changelogs and
app releases. The index answers a question investors, regulators, and journalists all want:
*who is talking versus building?* And for our program, it's the corporate wing of a theme we
keep meeting: claims about AI as social performance.

**Research questions.**
1. Measurement: construct a firm-quarter AI-talk score (frequency, specificity, and
   verifiability of AI language in calls/filings — vague "AI-powered" vs. named systems and
   metrics) and an AI-substance score (AI-role share of postings, AI patent flow, shipped
   features); the **wash score** is the standardized gap.
2. Consequences: does high wash predict (a) negative earnings surprises and long-run
   underperformance, (b) SEC enforcement or securities litigation, (c) quiet product
   abandonment (features announced then removed from marketing pages — detectable via Wayback
   diffs)?
3. Dynamics: did the 2024–2026 enforcement wave discipline the language — is talk becoming
   more specific, and in which sectors is washing worst?

**Method and data.** S&P 1500 panel, 2019–present: EDGAR full-text (10-K/10-Q/8-K), earnings
transcripts (IR pages and archives), Wayback snapshots of careers and product pages, USPTO
bulk data. NLP: specificity/verifiability scoring of AI claims (rubric + LLM-assisted with
hand-validated gold set); standard asset-pricing tests (calendar-time portfolios, controls)
for the returns question; hazard models for enforcement.

**Deliverable.** A finance/accounting paper (*Journal of Accounting and Economics*,
*Management Science*) plus the public "AI-Washing Index" — quarterly, sortable, receipt-linked.
Of everything we've pitched, this artifact has the clearest non-academic constituency
(investors and regulators) and the clearest headline potential.

**Feasibility and risks.** Medium-high. Transcript access is the main friction (EDGAR is free;
full call transcripts may need archived IR pages — scope v1 to firms with public IR archives
and say so). Returns tests must avoid data-snooping (pre-register the factor construction).
Legal exposure is manageable: the index reports measured gaps with receipts, not fraud
accusations — language matters and we will be careful ("high wash score," never "fraud").

**Why us.** It reuses the receipt-backed-scorecard machinery we're already building for
study #8, pointed at a richer target: the same accountability instrument, applied to money
rather than punditry.
